Negotiate your first raise

No savings hack compounds like income. A single successful negotiation early in your career raises every future raise.

111197 editorial team · Updated November 2026 · 7-minute read

The four parts

  • Know your number · build your case · use a script · time it right.

Why it matters so much

Raises are usually a percentage of your current pay. If you start $3,000 higher, a 3% annual raise is calculated on a bigger base every year after. Over a decade, that early gap typically grows into tens of thousands in extra earnings — and more for retirement contributions tied to salary.

1. Know your number

  • Check pay data for your role, level and city from government labour statistics, salary surveys and job postings that list ranges (many regions now require pay transparency in postings).
  • Pick three numbers: your walk-away (minimum acceptable), your target, and an anchor slightly above target.
  • Think total compensation: base pay, bonus, benefits, remote days, training budget and start date.

2. Build a one-page case

List three to five results with numbers: “Cut report time from two days to four hours,” “Handled 40 customer tickets a week with a 96% satisfaction score.” For a first job, use internships, projects, certifications and relevant skills — including practical AI tool skills that save the team time.

3. Use a script

For an offer: “Thank you — I’m excited about this role and the team. Based on my research for this position in [city] and the skills I bring in [X and Y], I was expecting something closer to [anchor]. Is there flexibility to get there?”

For a raise: “Over the last [period] I’ve [result 1], [result 2] and taken on [responsibility]. Market data for this role is around [range]. I’d like to discuss moving my salary to [target]. What would it take to get there?”

Then stop talking. Silence is part of the script. If the answer is “not now,” ask: “What specific results would make this a yes, and can we revisit on [date]?” — and put that date on your calendar (the 11th works).

4. Time it right

  • Offers: negotiate after the written offer, before you accept.
  • Raises: one to two months before budget planning or performance reviews — not during them.
  • After a visible win, not after a rough week.

What not to do

  • Don’t lead with personal expenses; lead with market value and results.
  • Don’t bluff about competing offers you don’t have.
  • Don’t accept on the spot — “Can I have until Friday to review?” is always reasonable.
